how it thinks

minimax search in quoridor

Every turn, the AI considers every legal move it could make. For each, it imagines the opponent's best response. For each of those, its own best reply. And so on. This is called minimax search. The deeper it looks, the stronger it plays — but the tree of possibilities grows explosively.

the search tree

Each row of the tree below shows one ply of lookahead. The root (top) is the current position. From it, dozens of branches spread out — one for each of the AI's legal moves. Each of those branches out again, for the opponent's replies. And so on.

The red path is the principal variation — the single best sequence of moves the AI found, assuming both players play optimally. Darker branches are ones the AI fully examined. The many faint grey branches are the ones alpha-beta pruned — it proved they couldn't lead to a better outcome than what it had already found, so it skipped them.

the complexity explosion

In the starting position of a 2-player game, the AI has 131 legal moves (3 pawn moves plus 128 legal wall placements). If the opponent also has ~131 replies, and so on, the tree of possibilities grows exponentially:

depth full tree with pruning + ordering
1 131 ~131
2 17,161 ~2,000
3 2,248,091 ~50,000
4 294,499,921 ~500,000
5 38,579,489,651 ~5,000,000

Full tree is how many positions we'd have to examine without any cleverness. With pruning + ordering is what we actually check: alpha-beta pruning skips entire branches once a better alternative is found, and wall-candidate filtering only considers walls near pawns or along opponent paths (not all 128). Combined, this is a reduction of tens of thousands of times at deeper levels.

log scale

Each deeper level takes roughly 5–10× longer than the previous one (note the bar chart is on a log scale to keep the smaller bars visible — the actual growth is exponential). That's why the AI caps at depth 4 with a time budget of 2 seconds — beyond that, moves would take too long. Hard difficulty typically reaches depth 3 in the opening and depth 4 in simpler mid/endgame positions.

why this plays well

The evaluation function at each leaf position is simple:

score = (opponent_distance − my_distance) × 10 + (my_walls − opp_walls) × 2

Two signals: am I closer to my goal than my opponent, and do I have more walls? No hand-crafted positional heuristics. The strength comes from search depth — looking 3–4 moves ahead captures most short-term tactics (wall forks, jumps, forced sequences), and the evaluation handles the long-term goal of reaching the other side.

About the weights: the 10× on distance and 2× on walls are not grounded in theory — they're a design choice. The relative ratio matters more than the absolute values (multiplying both by 100 changes nothing). What 10:2 says is that one step of path advantage is worth five walls in hand. That sounds extreme, but remember: the effect of actually placing a wall is captured directly by the search — if placing a wall lengthens the opponent's path by 3 steps, that shows up as +30 score during minimax. The 2× weight is only the latent value of having a wall in reserve for later use. A higher weight (say 8×) would make the AI hoard walls; a lower weight would make it wasteful. 2× is a reasonable middle ground picked by feel, not optimised.

A strong human player does essentially the same thing: they count path lengths, consider a few move-sequences, and pick the one that leaves them best off. The AI just does it more exhaustively.
